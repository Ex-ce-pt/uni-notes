
Core part of microbiology.
Allows us to see very small objects.
Series of lenses are used to magnify studied objects and sharpen the image.

> Some energy is lost in fluorescence, so, e.g. GFP (Green Fluorescent Protein) emits green light, even though it gets excited by UV light.

## Photographic terminology

**Magnification** - making an object appear `x` times larger than it actually is.
**Resolution** - how well the details of the object are visible.
**Numerical aperture** - lens's ability to capture light.
**Contrast** - how much the object stands out from the background.

The shorter the wavelength, the smaller objects it is possible to discern in a microscope.

## Light microscopy

Object is scanned with: **light**
Type: **common**
Expected magnification: **x40-x1000**

Can use knobs to adjust the position of the stage and intensity of the light.

**Immersion oil** - oil that has a refractive index similar to that of glass.
Used to avoid air bubbles in the sample from scattering light rays.

> Both the lens used and the eyepiece do magnify the object, the total magnification is the product of the two.
> Lens selected: x40, eyepiece: x10, total: x400

==Q: are brightfield and darkfield microscopes different pieces of equipment or is there a setting?

#### Brightfield

Light passes directly through the solution with the objects studied.
Bright background.
**Use stain**.
**Use immersion oil**.
**Works with dead microbes**.

#### Darkfield

The light going directly through the stage is blocked.
Light only touches on the edges of the objects studied.
Dark background.
High contrast.
**No stain needed**.
**No immersion oil needed**.
**Works with alive microbes**.

#### Phase-contrast

The oldest.
Light passing through the sample refracts light, light that doesn't pass gets blocked.
Can see organelles.

#### Differential interference contrast (DIC)

Uses 2 beams with different polarization.
Works similarly to [phase-contrast](#Phase-contrast).
Works with **live unstained specimen**.

#### Confocal

Type: **a couple per university**
Laser scans the sample in layers.
3D image reconstructed on the computer.
Good for **thick samples**.
**Stain often** used.

#### Two-photon

Type: **very expensive!**
Like [[#Confocal]], but low-energy light (red) is used, so 2 photons are needed for excitation.


## Electron

#### Scanning tunneling microscope (STM)

A tip is moved along the surface of the sample and the closer it gets, the more $e^-$ travel onto the sample.
The tip moves at the same height.
Usually **for conductors/semiconductors**, not biology.
x100 000 000 magnification!

#### Atomic force microscope (AFM)

A tip is moved along the surface of the sample.
The tip goes up and down with the surface of the sample.
Laser measures the bending of the tip, 3D image of the surface is constructed.

## Stains

**Fluorescence** - releasing photons almost immediately after getting into the excited state.
**Phosphorescence** - releasing photons after absorption *with a delay*.
**Fluorochromes** - fluorescent stains.

**Basic dye** - $(+)$-charged dye (like a protonated base).
**Acidic dye** - $(-)$-charged dye (like a deprotonated acid).

**Positive staining** - stains bind to the specimen and make it visible.
**Negative staining**  - stains **do not** bind to the specimen and the background is stained.

(Positively-staining) Stains can bind to various structures in a cell.
**Immunofluorescence** - uses anti-bodies and stains that bind to these anti-bodies.

**Differential staining** - staining different things different colors in the same sample, so they are easier to discern.
